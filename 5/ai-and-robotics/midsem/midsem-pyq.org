* Q1. Rotation representations & robot applications
a) Summarize any four alternate representation of rotations with respect to
singularity and number of parameters involved in rotation representation.
[CO1] [BTL2] [8M]
b) Name any three applications where robotics is being employed.
[CO1] [BTL1] [3M]

** Answer
- a) Four rotation representations: rotation matrix (9 params, no singularity),
  Euler angles (3 params, gimbal-lock singularity), roll-pitch-yaw / fixed angles
  (3 params, singularity), axis-angle / angle-axis (3-4 params, ambiguous at 0/180),
  quaternion (4 params, no singularity). Pick any four and state params + singularity.
- b) Three applications: industrial welding/assembly, surgical (da Vinci) robots,
  warehouse/logistics (AGV, picking), space/planetary rovers, disaster rescue,
  domestic/service robots.

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - A rotation is an element of SO(3): orthogonal matrix with det = +1.
  - Singularity = a configuration where the representation loses a degree of
    freedom / becomes non-unique (e.g. gimbal lock).
  - "Number of parameters" = minimal real numbers needed; >3 means redundancy
    that must satisfy constraints.
- Rotation matrix: 9 numbers, 6 constraints (orthonormal columns) -> 3 DOF; no
  singularity but verbose.
- Euler angles (Z-Y-X / Z-Y-Z etc.): 3 angles; singular when the middle rotation
  is +/-90 deg (gimbal lock) -> first and third axes align.
- Roll-pitch-yaw (fixed / nautical): 3 angles about fixed X,Y,Z; same gimbal-lock
  issue at pitch = +/-90 deg.
- Axis-angle (angle-axis): rotation by angle theta about unit axis k -> 3 params
  (k,theta) or 4 (kx,ky,kz,theta); singular/ambiguous at theta=0 and theta=180.
- Quaternion: \(q = [\cos(\theta/2),\ k \sin(\theta/2)]\) -> 4 params, 1 constraint
  (unit norm); no singularity; standard for interpolation.
- Application areas: recall CO1 "fundamentals + components" and the Section 1
  "Applications of AI in Robotics" list.

** Model Answer
- a) Tabulate any four:
  | Representation | # params | Singularity |
  |---|---|---|
  | Rotation matrix | 9 (3 DOF, 6 constraints) | none |
  | Euler angles | 3 | gimbal lock at middle angle = +/-90 deg |
  | Roll-pitch-yaw | 3 | gimbal lock at pitch = +/-90 deg |
  | Axis-angle | 3-4 | ambiguous at 0 deg and 180 deg |
  | Quaternion | 4 (unit) | none |
  (Any four rows accepted.)
- b) e.g. (i) industrial pick-and-place/ welding, (ii) surgical teleoperation
  (da Vinci), (iii) warehouse AGVs. [3M]

* Q4. Rotation matrices (fixed & moving frames)
Find the rotation matrices for the following cases: [CO2] [BTL3]
a) When rotation has happened with respect to \(y\)-axis by \(49^\circ\). [2M]
b) When rotation has happened with respect to \(x\)-axis by \(126^\circ\). [2M]
c) Rotation in \(x\)-axis by \(56^\circ\) with respect to fixed frame followed by
rotation in \(z\)-axis by \(27^\circ\) with respect to fixed frame and then followed
by rotation in \(x\)-axis by \(230^\circ\) with respect to fixed frame. [3M]
d) Rotation in \(z\)-axis by \(12^\circ\) with respect to moving frame followed by
rotation in \(y\)-axis by \(68^\circ\) with respect to fixed frame and then followed
by rotation in \(z\)-axis by \(78^\circ\) with respect to moving frame. [3M]

** Answer
- a) R_y(49) (fixed-frame about Y).
- b) R_x(126) (fixed-frame about X).
- c) Fixed-frame chain: R = R_x(56) R_z(27) R_x(230) (left-multiply order).
- d) Mixed: R = R_z(12) R_y(68) R_z(78) where first z is moving, y is fixed,
  final z is moving -> R = R_z(12) * R_y(68) * R_z(78) (pre-multiply fixed,
  post-multiply moving).

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - Basic rotation matrices \(R_x(\theta)\), \(R_y(\theta)\), \(R_z(\theta)\)
    (memorise the 3 forms).
  - Fixed / inertial frame rotations: compose by LEFT multiplication, order
    written left-to-right as applied: \(R = R_a R_b R_c\).
  - Moving / body frame rotations: compose by RIGHT (post) multiplication:
    \(R = R_a R_b R_c\) (\(R_a\) applied first, then b in new frame, then c).
  - Rule of thumb: "fixed frame -> pre-multiply; moving frame -> post-multiply."
\[
R_x(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&c&-s\\0&s&c\end{bmatrix}
\quad
R_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}c&0&s\\0&1&0\\-s&0&c\end{bmatrix}
\quad
R_z(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}c&-s&0\\s&c&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
\]
  with \(c=\cos\theta\), \(s=\sin\theta\).

** Model Answer
- a) \(R_y(49)\): \(c=\cos49=0.656\), \(s=\sin49=0.755\).
  \[
  R = \begin{bmatrix}0.656&0&0.755\\0&1&0\\-0.755&0&0.656\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  [2M]
- b) \(R_x(126)\): \(c=\cos126=-0.588\), \(s=\sin126=0.809\).
  \[
  R = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-0.588&-0.809\\0&0.809&-0.588\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  [2M]
- c) Fixed-frame, so pre-multiply in given order:
  \(R = R_x(56)\, R_z(27)\, R_x(230)\).
  Compute numerically or leave in this form. [3M]
- d) z(moving) -> post, y(fixed) -> pre, z(moving) -> post:
  \(R = R_z(12)\, R_y(68)\, R_z(78)\). [3M]

* Q5. Homogeneous transform + inverse
Find the homogeneous transformation matrix and its inverse when a robot has
rotated by \(34^\circ\) with respect to \(x\)-axis as well as translated to point
\((2, 1, 5)\) from origin. [CO2] [BTL3] [4M]

** Answer
- \(R = R_x(34)\), \(p = (2,1,5)\).
  \[
  T = \begin{bmatrix} R_x(34) & p \\ 0^T & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \]
  Inverse:
  \[
  T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T p \\ 0^T & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \]

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - Homogeneous transformation
    \[
    T = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0^T & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    combines rotation \(R\) and translation \(p\).
  - For a rotation+translation, the inverse uses \(R^{-1}=R^T\) (orthogonal) and
    moves the origin back:
    \[
    T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T p \\ 0^T & 1 \end{bmatrix}
    \]
  - \(R_x(\theta)\) form from Q4 concept.

** Model Answer
- \(R_x(34)\): \(c=\cos34=0.829\), \(s=\sin34=0.559\).
  \[
  T = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&2\\0&0.829&-0.559&1\\0&0.559&0.829&5\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  [2M]
- \(R^T = R_x(-34)\) (transpose flips the off-diagonal signs):
  \[
  R^T = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0.829&0.559\\0&-0.559&0.829\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  \[
  -R^T p = -\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0.829&0.559\\0&-0.559&0.829\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\5\end{bmatrix}
  = -\begin{bmatrix}2\\3.624\\3.586\end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\-3.624\\-3.586\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  \[
  T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&-2\\0&0.829&0.559&-3.624\\0&-0.559&0.829&-3.586\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
  \]
  [2M]

* Q6. Forward vs inverse kinematics (serial vs parallel)
Mention the differences between forward kinematics and inverse kinematics of
parallel as well as serial link robots. [CO2] [BTL2] [4M]

** Answer
- Forward kinematics (FK): given joint variables -> compute end-effector pose.
  Serial: straightforward chain multiplication, unique solution, closed form easy.
  Parallel: more complex, multiple loops, FK harder.
- Inverse kinematics (IK): given desired end-effector pose -> find joint
  variables. Serial: often multiple/no solutions, computationally hard.
  Parallel: often unique/closer solutions, IK relatively easier due to
  constraints.

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - Kinematics = study of motion without forces. FK maps configuration (joint
    angles) to task-space pose; IK is the inverse map.
  - Serial manipulator: open chain (links in series); each joint adds a transform.
  - Parallel manipulator: closed kinematic chain (e.g. Delta, Stewart platform);
    end-effector driven by several legs.
- Key difference: in serial robots FK is easy / IK hard (multiple solutions,
  singularities); in parallel robots the opposite tends to hold (FK harder
  because of coupled loops, IK simpler / more constrained).

** Model Answer
- Forward kinematics:
  - Serial: product of link transforms \(T = A_1 A_2 \cdots A_n\); unique, simple. [1M]
  - Parallel: coupled loops; FK involves solving loop-closure eqns; harder. [1M]
- Inverse kinematics:
  - Serial: may have 0/1/many solutions (elbow-up/down); needs iterative or
    algebraic solving; harder. [1M]
  - Parallel: constraints reduce solution count; IK often direct/unique; easier.
    [1M]

* Q7. Angular velocity composition across frames
Prove that angular velocities of coordinate frames \(\{1\}\), \(\{2\}\) and
\(\{3\}\) with arbitrary rotation is related by
\({}^1\omega^3 = {}^1\omega^2 + {}_2^1R {}^2\omega^3\)
(\({}^M\omega^N\) can be read as angular velocity of frame-\(N\) with respect to
frame-\(M\) and \({}_M^NR\) can be read as rotation of frame-\(N\) with respect to
frame-\(M\)). [CO2] [BTL3] [7M]

** Answer
- Using {2} as intermediate: angular velocity of frame 3 w.r.t {1} = angular
  velocity of {2} w.r.t {1} PLUS the angular velocity of {3} w.r.t {2}
  expressed in {1}. The second term is rotated by \({}_2^1R\), giving
  \({}^1\omega^3 = {}^1\omega^2 + {}_2^1R\,{}^2\omega^3\).

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - Angular velocity is a vector; when adding velocities measured in different
    frames, you must express them in the SAME frame.
  - Rotation matrix \({}_B^A R\) rotates a vector expressed in {B} into {A}:
    \({}^A v = {}_B^A R\, {}^B v\).
  - Angular velocity addition:
    \({}^A\omega^C = {}^A\omega^B + {}^A\omega^{C/B}\),
    where the relative part is expressed in {A}.
- This is the rotational analogue of relative linear velocity
  \(v_{C/A} = v_{B/A} + v_{C/B}\) (all in one frame).

** Model Answer
- Treat {2} as the intermediate frame. By definition,
  \({}^1\omega^3 = {}^1\omega^2 + {}^1\omega^{3/2}\),
  where \({}^1\omega^{3/2}\) is the angular velocity of {3} relative to {2}, but
  expressed in frame {1}.
- The relative angular velocity is natively expressed in {2}:
  \({}^2\omega^3\). To express it in {1}, rotate by \({}_2^1R\):
  \({}^1\omega^{3/2} = {}_2^1R\,{}^2\omega^3\).
- Substituting:
  \({}^1\omega^3 = {}^1\omega^2 + {}_2^1R\,{}^2\omega^3.\) \(\square\) [7M]

* Q8. Singularity and degree of freedom
Explain briefly how singularity and robot's degree of freedom are connected.
[CO2] [BTL2] [3M]

** Answer
- A robot is at a singularity when it loses the ability to move in some
  direction in task space (the Jacobian drops rank) even though it still has
  DOF. DOF = number of independently controllable joints; singularity is a
  configuration where those DOF cannot be used to produce arbitrary
  end-effector motion -> the manipulator is at a kinematic extreme (e.g. fully
  stretched or folded).

** Concept
- Prerequisites:
  - DOF = number of independently controlled joints/actuators (recall Part 1:
    up to 6 in 3D: X,Y,Z + roll,pitch,yaw).
  - The Jacobian \(J\) maps joint velocities to end-effector twist:
    \(\dot{x} = J \dot{q}\).
  - Singularity occurs when \(\mathrm{rank}(J) < \mathrm{DOF}\)
    (\(\det J = 0\) for square \(J\)): the robot
    cannot instantaneously move along some task-space direction.
  - Connection: DOF sets the dimension of reachable motion; a singularity is a
    *pose* where that full motion capability collapses locally.

** Model Answer
- Degree of freedom = count of independent joint variables (controllable DOF).
  [1M]
- The relationship between joint motion and end-effector motion is given by the
  Jacobian; at a singularity \(\mathrm{rank}(J) < \mathrm{DOF}\), so some
  end-effector directions
  become unreachable instantaneously even though the joints can still move. [1M]
- Thus a robot "has N DOF" but is at a singularity when those N DOF cannot
  produce arbitrary N-dimensional end-effector motion (e.g. arm fully extended
  or two axes aligned). Singularity is a configuration-level loss of mobility,
  not a loss of DOF. [1M]
