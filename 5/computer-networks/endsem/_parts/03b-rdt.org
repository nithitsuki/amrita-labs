** Reliable data transfer: the ladder from rdt 1.0 to rdt 3.0
SUMMARY: Each rdt version adds one mechanism to survive one more defect in the channel.
KUROSE: 3.4.1, 3.4.2
EXAM: not asked as a direct question, but every GBN and SR answer rests on this ladder.
PRIORITY: core

| Version | Assumed channel     | Mechanisms added                        | Remaining problem              |
|---------+---------------------+-----------------------------------------+--------------------------------|
| rdt 1.0 | no errors, no loss  | none                                    | nothing, channel is perfect    |
| rdt 2.0 | bit errors, no loss | checksum, ACK and NAK, stop and wait    | a corrupted ACK or NAK         |
| rdt 2.1 | bit errors, no loss | 1-bit seq number, duplicate detection   | no loss handling, NAK needed   |
| rdt 2.2 | bit errors, no loss | ACK carries the last good seq number    | no loss handling               |
| rdt 3.0 | errors and loss     | countdown timer, retransmit on timeout  | correct, but very slow         |

rdt 3.0 sends one packet then waits a full round trip, so a long fat link sits
idle almost all the time.

** Stop and wait, and the utilisation problem
SUMMARY: Stop and wait wastes the link because the sender idles for one round trip after every packet.
KUROSE: 3.4.1, 3.4.2 (Figure 3.20)
EXAM: "why is pipelining better than stop and wait", 5 marks, CNE1 and CNE3.
PRIORITY: core

*** The utilisation formula
Utilisation is the fraction of time the sender is actually pushing bits.

  U = (L/R) / (RTT + L/R)

  L/R   = transmission time, the time to push all L bits onto the link
  RTT   = round trip time, data out plus ACK back
  L/R + RTT = one full cycle of stop and wait

The numerator is the busy part. The denominator is the whole cycle.

*** The timeline
#+begin_example
 time          sender                       receiver
  |              |                           |
  t=0            |== first bit leaves ======>|
                 |                           |
  t=L/R          |== last bit leaves         |
                 |        . . . . . . . . . >|
  t=L/R+prop     |                           | last bit lands
                 |                           | ACK sent at once
                 |   idle, nothing in flight |
                 |<================ ACK =====|
  t=L/R+RTT      |  ACK lands, send next     |
                 |== next data =============>|
#+end_example

The idle band is the whole problem. No data crosses the link during it.

*** WORKED EXAMPLE (numbers changed on purpose)
A 1 Mbps link, 1500 byte packets, one way propagation delay 25 ms.

  L = 1500 x 8 = 12000 bits
  L/R = 12000 / 1000000 = 0.012 s = 12 ms
  RTT = 2 x 25 ms = 50 ms
  U = 12 / (50 + 12) = 12 / 62 = 0.1935

The sender uses 19.4 percent of the link. The other 80.6 percent is idle.

*** Pipelining fixes it
Let the sender keep N packets in flight instead of one. The busy part of the
cycle becomes N x L/R and the cycle length does not change.

  U = (N x L/R) / (RTT + L/R)

  with N = 5:  U = (5 x 12) / 62 = 60 / 62 = 0.968

Utilisation rose from 19.4 percent to 96.8 percent. Pipelining multiplies
utilisation by N, up to the point where the link is full.

The link is full when N packets take at least one RTT to transmit.

  N >= (RTT + L/R) / (L/R)      here N >= 62 / 12 = 5.17

So N = 6 fills this pipe. Past that, extra packets only build a queue.

*** Trap: using the one way delay where RTT belongs
The confusion: the student writes U = (L/R) / (prop + L/R) and gets double the
true answer. The fix: the ACK must travel back, so the idle time is a round
trip, which is two propagation delays. Compute RTT first, then use it.

*** Trap: thinking a bigger RTT means a faster link
The confusion: a long link looks impressive, so the student expects high
utilisation. The fix: a long link makes RTT large and the denominator large, so
utilisation falls. Distance hurts stop and wait and helps nobody.

*** Trap: L/R and RTT are both times, so adding them is safe but swapping is not
The confusion: the student divides RTT by L/R. The fix: the ratio of busy time
to total time is the only meaningful fraction. Keep L/R on top.

** Go-Back-N
SUMMARY: GBN keeps a window of N packets in flight and the receiver throws away anything out of order.
KUROSE: 3.4.3
EXAM: GBN against SR with sequence diagrams, 5 marks, CNE3 and CNE6. The GBN rate numerical is also CNE3.
PRIORITY: core

*** The sender
The sender holds a window of up to N consecutive packets that are sent but not
yet acknowledged. Three variables run it.

  send_base    = oldest packet sent and not yet ACKed
  next_seqnum  = next packet to send
  window       = the range [send_base, send_base + N - 1]

Rules:

- If next_seqnum is inside the window, send the packet and advance next_seqnum.
- If next_seqnum is outside the window, refuse the data and tell the app later.
- One timer only, and it belongs to the oldest unACKed packet.
- On timeout for packet n, resend n and every higher packet in the window.
- ACKs are cumulative. ACK(n) means every packet up to and including n landed.

Cumulative ACK is the reason for one timer. A single ACK number moves the base
forward and implicitly clears many packets at once.

*** The receiver
The receiver keeps one variable, rcv_base, the next packet it expects in order.

- Packet with seq == rcv_base: deliver it, advance rcv_base, send ACK(rcv_base-1).
- Packet with any other seq: discard it, send ACK(rcv_base-1) again.
- No buffering and no reordering. The receiver never stores a gap filler.

That duplicate ACK is not a request. It is a report of the highest in-order
packet seen, repeated.

*** Sequence diagram, window of 4, packet 1 lost
#+begin_example
 time    sender                            receiver
  |        |                                 |
  t1       |--- pkt0 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 0
           |<---------------------- ACK0 ----| base=1, window [1..4]
  t2       |--- pkt1 ---- X LOST ------------|
  t3       |--- pkt2 ---------------------->| out of order, discard
           |<---------------------- ACK0 ----| duplicate
  t4       |--- pkt3 ---------------------->| out of order, discard
           |<---------------------- ACK0 ----| duplicate
  t5       |--- pkt4 ---------------------->| out of order, discard
           |<---------------------- ACK0 ----| dup, ignored
  t6       |  timer for pkt1 fires           |
  t7       |--- pkt1 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 1
           |<---------------------- ACK1 ----|
  t8       |--- pkt2 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 2
           |<---------------------- ACK2 ----|
  t9       |--- pkt3 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 3
           |<---------------------- ACK3 ----|
  t10      |--- pkt4 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 4
           |<---------------------- ACK4 ----|
#+end_example

Read the discard lines carefully. Packets 2, 3 and 4 arrive intact and are
still thrown away. Then all four are sent again.

Count the waste. Five useful packets, nine transmissions. Packets 2, 3, 4 were
sent twice and packet 1 was sent twice.

*** Window size limit
Sequence numbers are k bits wide, so the space is 2^k values.

  N <= 2^k - 1

  k = 3 bits, space 8, so N <= 7

One value must stay unused. If the window covered all 2^k values, then ACK(n)
and ACK(n - 2^k) would name the same number and the sender could not tell an
old ACK from a new one.

*** Efficiency
GBN is efficient while nothing is lost. After a loss it wastes bandwidth equal
to the number of packets sent after the lost one, because all of them are sent
again. A loss at the start of a large window costs almost a whole window.

*** Trap: treating the duplicate ACK as a negative ACK
The confusion: the student says the receiver "NAKs packet 2" when packet 2 is
missing. The fix: GBN has no NAK. The receiver repeats ACK0, which means "I
still want 1". The sender learns nothing new from it and waits for its timer.

*** Trap: moving the window on a duplicate ACK
The confusion: every ACK0 slides the window. The fix: ACK(n) slides the base
only when n >= send_base. A stale ACK below the base is ignored. In the diagram
the last ACK0 changes nothing.

*** Trap: N = 2^k instead of 2^k - 1
The confusion: 3 bit numbers give 8 values, so the window is 8. The fix: at
N = 2^k the base and the newest packet share a sequence number. Use 2^k - 1.

** Selective Repeat
SUMMARY: SR retransmits only the lost packet because the receiver buffers everything that arrives early.
KUROSE: 3.4.4
EXAM: paired with GBN in CNE3 and CNE6, 5 marks, usually asked as compare with sequence diagrams.
PRIORITY: core

*** The sender
The sender keeps a window of N packets and one logical timer per unACKed packet.

- Data from above: send if the sequence number is inside the window.
- Timeout(n): resend only packet n, restart its timer.
- ACK(n) inside the window: mark n as received.
- If n == send_base, advance the base past the run of acknowledged packets.

Individual ACKs mean the base can jump several places at once when a burst of
ACKs lands together.

*** The receiver
The receiver keeps a window of N packets, not one variable.

- Packet with seq inside the window: send ACK(n), buffer it if it is not in order.
- Packet that completes a run: deliver it plus every buffered packet after it,
  then slide the window to the first packet still missing.
- Packet with seq in the previous window, that is [rcv_base - N, rcv_base - 1]:
  send ACK(n) again. That is a duplicate, already delivered.
- Packet anywhere else: ignore it silently.

Buffering is the whole point. Data arrives early and waits for the gap.

*** Sequence diagram, window of 4, packet 1 lost
#+begin_example
 time    sender                            receiver
  |        |                                 |
  t1       |--- pkt0 ---------------------->| in order, deliver 0
           |<---------------------- ACK0 ----|
  t2       |--- pkt1 ---- X LOST ------------|
  t3       |--- pkt2 ---------------------->| buffer 2
           |<---------------------- ACK2 ----|
  t4       |--- pkt3 ---------------------->| buffer 3
           |<---------------------- ACK3 ----|
  t5       |--- pkt4 ---------------------->| buffer 4
           |<---------------------- ACK4 ----|
  t6       |  only the timer for pkt1 fires  |
  t7       |--- pkt1 ---------------------->| deliver 1, then 2, 3, 4
           |<---------------------- ACK1 ----| window slides to 5
#+end_example

Compare with GBN. Here the sender waits with 2, 3, 4 already acknowledged and
resends one packet. GBN resent four.

*** Window size rule and why
  N <= 2^(k-1)         that is 2N <= 2^k

  k = 3 bits, space 8, so N <= 4

Here is why. If 2N exceeds the sequence space, the two windows overlap on the
same sequence numbers after a slide. A late duplicate then looks like new
data, because the number it carries is one the receiver now expects.

*** Trap: thinking the receiver window slides on every packet
The confusion: each buffered packet moves the window. The fix: the window moves
only when rcv_base itself arrives and the buffered run behind it can be
delivered. Buffering alone changes nothing.

*** Trap: accepting a packet from the previous window as new data
The confusion: any packet inside the receiver window is new. The fix: the
window spans N numbers, but the previous N numbers are still legal on the wire.
A packet in [rcv_base - N, rcv_base - 1] is a duplicate and gets an ACK, not a
delivery.

*** Trap: "selective repeat has no timers on the receiver"
The confusion: only TCP-like senders time out. The fix: SR needs one timer per
in-flight packet on the sender side. The receiver has no timers at all. It
never retransmits and it never asks.

** GBN versus SR comparison
SUMMARY: GBN is simple and cheap in state, SR is efficient and expensive in state.
KUROSE: 3.4.3, 3.4.4
EXAM: evaluate the advantages and drawbacks of GBN and SR, 5 marks, CNE6. CNE3 asks for the same comparison with diagrams.
PRIORITY: core

| Property          | Go-Back-N                    | Selective Repeat              |
|-------------------+------------------------------+-------------------------------|
| Timers on sender  | one, for the oldest packet   | one per unACKed packet        |
| ACK type          | cumulative                   | individual per packet         |
| Receiver buffer   | none, discards the gap       | buffers out of order packets  |
| Retransmit cost   | lost packet and all after it | only the lost packet          |
| Complexity        | low, few variables           | high, per packet state        |
| Window limit      | N <= 2^k - 1                 | N <= 2^(k-1)                  |
| Best use case     | short link, low loss         | long link, costly resend      |

The verdict, ready to write in the exam. GBN wins on simplicity because one
variable and one timer run the whole receiver. SR wins on bandwidth because a
single loss costs one retransmission instead of a window. Choose GBN for short
low loss links and SR when the bandwidth delay product is large.

*** Trap: claiming SR always beats GBN
The confusion: fewer retransmissions must mean a faster protocol. The fix: SR
pays for that with per packet timers, per packet buffers and more header work.
On a short link with rare loss, GBN delivers the same rate for less effort.

*** Trap: "GBN buffers out of order packets"
The confusion: the Kurose slide says the receiver "can discard or buffer".
The fix: buffering is allowed as an implementation choice but it is useless,
because GBN still retransmits the whole window. The reference behaviour
delivers in order only and discards the gap.

** Pipelining: the maximum data rate numerical
SUMMARY: If the window cannot fill the pipe the rate is window over cycle time, otherwise it is just the bandwidth.
KUROSE: 3.4.2, 3.4.3
EXAM: CNE3 asks this with a 20 kbps satellite link, 400 ms delay, 100 byte frames and n = 10. Expect the same shape with new numbers.
PRIORITY: core

*** The method
The sender may have at most n frames outstanding. One cycle is: send n frames
back to back, then wait for the first ACK to return.

  cycle time = RTT + L/R

The ACK for frame 1 arrives one round trip plus one transmission time after
frame 1 left. Everything the sender fits inside that cycle sets the rate.

*** Two cases
Case 1, the window is larger than the pipe. The sender never runs dry. The link
stays busy and the rate is simply R.

Case 2, the window is smaller than the pipe. The sender goes idle every cycle.
The rate is the data per cycle divided by the cycle time.

  rate = n x L / (RTT + L/R)

*** The pipe
Two different quantities get called the pipe. Keep them apart.

  bits in flight on the wire   R x d_prop    this is the bandwidth-delay product
  bits needed to cover a trip  R x RTT       this is the window you need

  frames to fill the window    (R x RTT) / L

The sender needs enough frames outstanding to cover a round trip, because it
cannot send the next window until the first ACK returns. So window sizing uses
RTT, while the true in-flight capacity of the wire uses the one-way delay.

Compare n with the frame count from RTT. If n is at least that count, use
case 1. If n is smaller, use case 2.

*** WORKED EXAMPLE (numbers changed on purpose)
A 64 kbps link, one way propagation delay 250 ms, frame size 200 bytes,
window n = 8.

Step 1, convert everything.
  R = 64000 bps
  L = 200 x 8 = 1600 bits
  one way delay = 250 ms, so RTT = 2 x 250 = 500 ms = 0.5 s

Step 2, transmission time.
  L/R = 1600 / 64000 = 0.025 s = 25 ms

Step 3, cycle time.
  RTT + L/R = 500 + 25 = 525 ms = 0.525 s

Step 4, pipe size.
  R x RTT = 64000 x 0.5 = 32000 bits

Step 5, frames that fill the pipe.
  32000 / 1600 = 20 frames

Step 6, decide the case.
  n = 8 < 20, so the window is smaller than the pipe. Use case 2.

Step 7, rate.
  rate = 8 x 1600 / 0.525 = 12800 / 0.525 = 24381 bps

Step 8, utilisation.
  U = 24381 / 64000 = 0.381

The answer is about 24.4 kbps, or 38 percent of the link. The window is the
limit, not the link. Raising n to 20 would reach the full 64 kbps.

*** Trap: using case 1 when the window is small
The confusion: the paper gives a bandwidth, so the answer must be that
bandwidth. The fix: always compute the pipe first. A small window caps the rate
below R, and the answer is n x L over the cycle.

*** Trap: forgetting that RTT is twice the one way delay
The confusion: the paper says "propagation delay 400 ms", so RTT is 400 ms.
The fix: that phrasing means one way. Double it. Every rate then comes out
twice as high as it should.

*** Trap: comparing n with 2^k instead of with the pipe
The confusion: n = 10 with 3 bit numbers looks legal, so the link must be full.
The fix: the window limit and the pipe limit are different tests. The window
limit is a correctness test, the pipe comparison is a speed test.

*** Trap: mixing bits and bytes in the rate
The confusion: 200 byte frames with R in bits per second. The fix: convert L to
bits before dividing. A rate in bytes per second must be multiplied by 8 at the
end.

** Congestion control: principles
SUMMARY: Congestion control protects the network from too much offered load, while flow control protects one receiver from one fast sender.
KUROSE: 3.6, 3.6.1, 3.6.2
EXAM: the end to end versus network assisted split sits inside the CNE5 congestion question, 5 marks.
PRIORITY: extra

Flow control guards one receiver from one sender. Congestion control guards
the network from every sender at once. A slow receiver means flow control. A
busy network means congestion control.

Two causes. Too many sources send too fast. Routers have finite buffers, so
queues overflow and packets drop. Then senders retransmit, offered load rises,
and useful throughput falls. That fall is congestion collapse.

Two approaches. End to end control infers congestion from loss and delay, as
TCP does. Network assisted control takes explicit feedback from routers, as in
ECN.

*** Trap: swapping congestion control and flow control
The confusion: both are called "control" and both limit the sender. The fix:
ask who is being protected. A slow receiver means flow control. A busy network
means congestion control. The window that binds is min(cwnd, rwnd).

** TCP congestion control
SUMMARY: TCP grows a congestion window until loss, halves it, and repeats, which gives the AIMD sawtooth.
KUROSE: 3.7, 3.7.1
EXAM: explain TCP end to end congestion control in detail, 5 marks, CNE5. The throughput numerical is a standard add on.
PRIORITY: core

*** The window that governs sending
TCP keeps a congestion window cwnd, measured in bytes or MSS.

  effective window = min(cwnd, rwnd)
  in flight limit  : LastByteSent - LastByteAcked <= cwnd

rwnd is the buffer space the receiver advertises, so it bounds flow control.
cwnd is the guess the sender makes about the network, so it bounds
congestion. Whichever is smaller wins.

*** Slow start
The connection begins with cwnd = 1 MSS. Every ACK increments cwnd, so cwnd
doubles once per RTT.

  RTT 1: 1 MSS
  RTT 2: 2 MSS
  RTT 3: 4 MSS
  RTT 4: 8 MSS

This is exponential. It is called slow start only because the first step is
small. The ramp is fast, and it ends at ssthresh.

  on a loss: ssthresh = cwnd / 2 at the moment of loss

*** Congestion avoidance
Once cwnd reaches ssthresh, growth becomes additive.

  cwnd = cwnd + 1 MSS per RTT

One extra segment each round trip. Together with a halving on loss, this is
AIMD, additive increase and multiplicative decrease.

*** The sawtooth
#+begin_example
  W  |                        /*   peak, then loss
     |                      /  |
     |                    /    |
     |                 /       |
     |               /         |
 ssh |             /           |         /  ssthresh = W/2
     |           /             |       /
     |         /               |     /
     |      /                  |   /
     |    /                    |  /
  1  |  /                      | /
     +---------------------------------------------------> time
        slow start   congestion avoidance    timeout
#+end_example

Growth is exponential, then linear, then a cliff. The cliff is the loss event.
The pattern repeats, and that repeated probe is the sawtooth.

*** Loss detection, Tahoe and Reno
A timeout is severe. The network gave no ACKs at all for a long time.

  timeout:  ssthresh = cwnd / 2,  cwnd = 1,  re-enter slow start

Three duplicate ACKs are mild. Packets are still flowing, so one packet may
have been lost. The receiver keeps repeating the last in-order ACK, and each
repeat names the same missing byte.

  three duplicate ACKs:  fast retransmit, resend the missing segment at once
  TCP Tahoe:  any loss, including three duplicates, drops cwnd to 1
  TCP Reno:   timeout drops cwnd to 1, three duplicates use fast recovery

Fast recovery in Reno sets ssthresh = cwnd / 2 and cwnd = ssthresh + 3 MSS.
The three extra segments stand for the three packets that already left the
network. Each further duplicate ACK inflates cwnd by one MSS. The new ACK
deflates cwnd back to ssthresh and congestion avoidance resumes.

*** Throughput
Two forms of the same idea, one in terms of window, one in terms of loss.

  throughput = 0.75 x W / RTT

W is the window size in bytes at the moment of loss. The 0.75 comes from
averaging the sawtooth: the window falls from W to W/2, so the mean is 0.75W.

  throughput = 1.22 x MSS / (RTT x sqrt(p))

p is the loss probability. This form comes from the square root of p rule: the
window a flow settles at scales as 1 over sqrt(p).

*** WORKED EXAMPLE (numbers changed on purpose)
MSS = 1460 bytes, RTT = 80 ms, loss probability p = 0.02.

Step 1, take the square root of p.
  sqrt(0.02) = 0.1414

Step 2, build the denominator.
  RTT x sqrt(p) = 0.08 x 0.1414 = 0.011314 s

Step 3, build the numerator.
  1.22 x 1460 = 1781.2 bytes

Step 4, divide.
  throughput = 1781.2 / 0.011314 = 157437 bytes per second

Step 5, convert to bits.
  157437 x 8 = 1259500 bps, about 1.26 Mbps

Check with the window form. The same loss rate implies W of about 12 MSS.
  0.75 x 12 x 1460 / 0.08 = 13140 / 0.08 = 164250 bytes per second

That is 1.31 Mbps. The two forms agree to within rounding, which is expected.
A 12 MSS window and a 2 percent loss rate describe the same sawtooth.

*** Trap: dropping cwnd to half on a timeout
The confusion: multiplicative decrease halves the window, so a timeout halves
it. The fix: only the three duplicate ACK case halves the window into fast
recovery. A timeout is severe, so cwnd returns to 1 MSS and slow start restarts.

*** Trap: calling ssthresh the current window
The confusion: ssthresh and cwnd are two names for the same value. The fix:
ssthresh is the switch point, a memory of the last loss. cwnd is the live
window. They are equal only at the instant slow start hands over.

*** Trap: reading three duplicate ACKs as a NAK
The confusion: three duplicates look like a request for retransmission. The fix:
they are one repeated ACK from a receiver that keeps getting out of order data.
The sender reads the repetition itself as a loss signal.

*** Trap: forgetting that p sits inside a square root
The confusion: throughput halves when loss doubles. The fix: throughput falls
with the square root of p, so it drops by about 1.41 when p doubles. A fourfold
rise in loss only halves the rate.

*** Trap: mixing units inside the throughput formula
The confusion: MSS in bytes with RTT in milliseconds. The fix: put RTT in
seconds and MSS in bytes. The answer then comes out in bytes per second, and you
multiply by 8 only if the question asks for bits.

** Drill answers, data transfer and congestion
SUMMARY: Model answers with the reasoning that the examiner looks for.
KUROSE: 3.4.2, 3.4.3, 3.4.4, 3.7.1
EXAM: not asked, this section is for self checking only.
PRIORITY: core

*** Drill 1 answers
  L = 1000 x 8 = 8000 bits
  L/R = 8000 / 2000000 = 0.004 s = 4 ms
  RTT = 2 x 40 ms = 80 ms
  cycle = 84 ms

  a) U = 4 / 84 = 0.0476, that is 4.8 percent
  b) pipe = R x RTT = 2000000 x 0.08 = 160000 bits
     frames = 160000 / 8000 = 20 frames
  c) U = (20 x 4) / 84 = 80 / 84 = 0.952, that is 95.2 percent
  d) rate = 10 x 8000 / 0.084 = 80000 / 0.084 = 952381 bps

Note for part d. A window of 10 is half the pipe, so the rate is about half of
2 Mbps. The small shortfall is the transmission time that the cycle still holds.

*** Drill 2 answers
a) ACK0, ACK1, ACK2, then four duplicate ACK2. The duplicates come from
packets 4, 5, 6 and 7, each discarded as out of order. Just one duplicate
follows packet 4, and one more follows each of 5, 6 and 7.

b) The window is [3..7] at the timeout, so the sender resends 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7.
GBN resends everything from the lost packet to the top of the window.

c) 8 first transmissions plus 5 retransmissions is 13. The retransmitted 4, 5,
6 and 7 were already sent once and thrown away, so 5 slots of bandwidth are
wasted.

*** Drill 3 answers
a) 3 bits gives 8 numbers. 2N = 10 is greater than 8, so it is illegal. The
largest legal window is 4.
b) 5 bits gives 32 numbers. 2N = 32 equals 32, so it is legal. It sits exactly
on the limit.
c) 4 bits gives 16 numbers. 2N = 18 is greater than 16, so it is illegal. The
largest legal window is 8.

*** Drill 4 answers
a) RTT 1 to 12: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 1, 2, 4.

Read the trace. RTT 1 to 5 is slow start, doubling from 1 to 16. RTT 5 lands
exactly on ssthresh, so RTT 6 onward adds one per round trip. The timeout hits
when cwnd is 20, so the window collapses to 1 and the pattern restarts.

b) New ssthresh = 20 / 2 = 10 MSS.
c) RTT 13 is 8, RTT 14 is 10, RTT 15 is 11. Slow start doubles until it reaches
ssthresh at 10, then congestion avoidance adds one per round trip.

Watch the handover. A double of 8 would be 16, but ssthresh is 10, so cwnd stops
at 10 and switches to additive growth. That is the step most answers get wrong.
