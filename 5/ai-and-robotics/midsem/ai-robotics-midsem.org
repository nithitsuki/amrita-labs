** What is a Robot?
- Robot: a machine capable of carrying out a complex series of actions
  automatically, especially one programmable by a computer (Wikipedia).
- Robot Institute of America (1979): a reprogrammable, multifunctional
  manipulator designed to move material, parts, tools or specialised devices
  through variable programmed motions for a variety of tasks.
- Robotics: the branch of technology dealing with the design, construction,
  operation and application of robots, plus the computer systems for control,
  sensory feedback and information processing (Wikipedia).

** History of Robotics
- 13th-15th c.: automatons (automated clockwork rooster, automatic scribe that
  could write ~40 characters, robotic woman playing a piano).
- 1898: Nikola Tesla reveals a wireless-controlled torpedo he calls
  "teleautomation".
- 1921: Karel Capek's play RUR (Rossum's Universal Robots) coins the word
  "Robot" (Robota = worker, Slavic); machines take over the world.
- 1942: Asimov's Laws of Robotics - do not harm humans; obey human orders that
  do not violate law 1; protect own existence if it does not conflict with 1/2.
- George Devol designs the first programmable robot, coins "Universal
  Automation" -> shortens to Unimation, the first robot company (1962).
- 1978: PUMA (Programmable Universal Machine for Assembly) developed by
  Unimation with GM design support.
- 1971: Soviet Union's Mars rover - the first interplanetary robot (technically
  it crashed on Mars).

** Components of a Robot
- Actuators / limbs (muscles), sensors, controller (brain), power conversion
  unit, user interface, manipulator linkage, base (fixed or movable).
- Human-sense analogues: speaker+mouth (voice), microphone (ears), odor sensor
  (nose), camera (eyes).

** Basic Terminologies
- Body: a graph of links and joints; wheel design; other mechanical parts;
  stability ratio for a basic rover chassis is 16:9:5.
- Senses robots need: measure robot configuration/condition and environment -
  beyond the five human senses.
- Actuators: DC motor, servo motor, stepper motor, brushless motor.
- Controller: the programmable "brain" that determines and controls functioning.
- Power: on-board or off-board; typically 3V-24V DC.

** Degree of Freedom (DoF)
- DoF = number of independently controlled actuators/joints; can be very high
  for many-jointed robots.
- A point in space needs X, Y, Z (translation) + roll, pitch, yaw (orientation)
  = 6 DoF (limited to 6 in 3D).
- A car has 3 DoF (translation in two directions + rotation) but only 2
  controllable (forward/reverse + steering) - cannot move sideways.
- Holonomic: controllable DoF = total DoF. Non-holonomic: controllable < total.
  Redundant: controllable > total.

* Part 2 - Position, Orientation & Pose in 2D (Section 2.1)

** A Point in 2D
- A point P on the plane is the simplest object to describe.
- In the Cartesian system, P is located by perpendicular projections onto the
  X and Y axes, giving coordinates (a, b) -> P = (a, b).
- The same point is equivalently a position vector p from the origin O to P:
  p = [a, b] (x-component a, y-component b).

** An Object in 2D Space
- A rigid object (e.g. a car) is placed on the X-Y plane with origin O.
- A body frame {B} (axes x_B, y_B) is attached to the object, centred at point B
  and oriented with the object.
- Points fixed on the body are described in {B}, then mapped to the world frame.
- An object needs BOTH a position and an orientation - together these are its pose.

* Part 3 - Position, Orientation & Pose in 3D (Section 2.2)

** Point in 3D
- Right-handed 3D Cartesian frame X, Y, Z from origin O. Counter-clockwise =
  positive rotation sense. Generalises the 2D point to 3 coordinates.

#+CAPTION: Right-handed 3D Cartesian coordinate system
[[file:_work/img/04/page-04.png]]

** Pose in 3D
- Pose = position + orientation of a frame relative to a reference frame.
- Rotation about an axis follows the right-hand rule: thumb along the axis,
  fingers curl in the positive-rotation direction.

#+CAPTION: Right-hand rule for rotation about an axis
[[file:_work/img/04/page-11.png]]

** Rotation in 3D
- In 2D, rotation is about the hidden Z axis.
- A rotation matrix has columns that are the new (rotated) axes; each column is
  a unit vector and orthogonal to the others.
- The matrix transforms vectors from frame {B} to frame {A}.
- The inverse of a rotation matrix equals its transpose; determinant is +1.

** Rotations are Non-Commutative
- Two sequences of pi/2 rotations give different final orientations (e.g. about
  Z then Y vs about X then Z). Order matters - rotation matrices do not commute.

** Analysing Manipulator Moves in 3D - PUMA 560
- Labelled PUMA 560: links 0-6, joints 1-6, base frame x0, y0, z0. Used to map
  physical links/joints onto frames for forward kinematics.

** Forward Kinematics & the SE(n) Group
- Forward kinematics computes the end-effector pose from the joint variables.
- The 2D transforms (translation Tx/Ty, rotation R) are elements of SE(2);
  the 3D transforms (Tx/Ty/Tz, Rx/Ry/Rz) are elements of SE(3).
- Robot configuration is the vector of joint variables; the configuration space
  C has dimension = number of joints (DoF).
- The task space is the set of all possible end-effector poses; a robot can
  achieve any position/orientation and control the path when it has enough DoF.

** Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) Parameters (Section 3.2)
- Standardised convention (introduced 1955) to attach a coordinate frame to
  each link and describe the chain with a minimal parameter set.
- Each joint is attached, via a link, to the previous one.
- The result uses only 4 parameters per link (not 6), describing how each joint
  axis relates to the next.
- The DH table completely describes the robot's kinematics; it is implemented as
  an array of SE(3) transforms indexed along the link chain.

* Part 5 - Intro to AI (presentation)

** What is Artificial Intelligence?
- John McCarthy: "the science and engineering of making intelligent machines,
  especially intelligent computer programs... related to using computers to
  understand human intelligence, but AI need not confine itself to biologically
  observable methods."
- Intelligence: the computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the
  world; occurs in varying degrees in people, animals and some machines.
- Views of AI fall into four categories:
  - Thinking humanly (simulate human thought) - introspection, GPS (Newell & Simon).
  - Thinking rationally - formal logic / "laws of thought".
  - Acting humanly - the Turing Test (A. M. Turing, 1950): requires natural
    language, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, machine learning,
    and (for the full test) vision & robotics.
  - Acting rationally - achieve goals with a performance measure.
- AI sub-fields: human-like simulation, mathematical problems (puzzles, games,
  theorems), common-sense reasoning, expert knowledge (law, medicine, diagnosis),
  social behaviour, rational goal achievement.

** Types of AI (nested scope)
- Artificial Intelligence is the broad set; Machine Learning is a subset;
  Deep Learning is a subset of Machine Learning.

* Part 6 - Pose & Rotation Matrices (presentation)

** Pose of a Rigid Body
- A rigid body is completely defined in space by its position and orientation
  with respect to a reference (inertial / ground frame).
- Notation: position vector of frame 0' with respect to frame 0.
- Tools to represent pose: Homogeneous Transformation Matrix, DH Convention,
  DH Parameters, and homogeneous transforms built from DH parameters.

** Rotation Matrix
- Using direction cosines (projection method) a rotation needs 9 variables.
- Using Euler angles (roll, pitch, yaw) it needs only 3 variables.
- A point P observed from frame B, and the transformation between frames
  {A} and {B}, are expressed via these rotation/transformation matrices.

* Summary - Key Formulas & Facts
- Point in 2D: P = (a, b), position vector p = [a, b]^T.
- 2D rotation by theta is about hidden Z; 3D rotation matrix R is orthogonal,
  R^{-1} = R^T, det(R) = +1.
- Rotations do not commute: order of rotations changes the result.
- Pose = (position, orientation); rigid body fully described by both.
- DoF: translational (X,Y,Z) + rotational (roll, pitch, yaw) = up to 6 in 3D.
- Holonomic = controllable DoF == total DoF; non-holonomic < ; redundant >.
- 2R planar arm: elbow-up and elbow-down both reach the same end point.
- DH parameters: 4 per link (standardised 1955), table fully describes kinematics.
- SE(2) = 2D rigid transforms (Tx,Ty,R); SE(3) = 3D rigid transforms.
- AI scope: AI > ML > Deep Learning.
